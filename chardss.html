The idea

Both players draw from one shared deck. Each turn you may play one card, then make one move, then draw back to three cards.

Every card changes exactly one rule, for one turn or one move. Your Pawns capture sideways. Your King swaps places with a Rook. Your opponent has to take back their last move. All normal chess rules still apply unless a card says otherwise.

Your losses pay for your cards

Problem
If powers are earned by playing well, the player who's already ahead gets stronger and the game snowballs.
Solution
You earn points when your own pieces are captured, equal to their chess value: Pawn 1, Knight and Bishop 3, Rook 5, Queen 9. You don't earn anything for capturing.
Result
The player who is behind on material has the most to spend. Most cards cost 1 or 2, so losing a Pawn or two already buys a real option. Comebacks are part of the resource system instead of luck.

Pricing the cards

Costs run from 0 to 39. Almost everything sits between 0 and 3, so cards come out often:

A family of cards lets your Pawns move like another piece for a turn. Their costs follow the strength of that piece: Kingified costs 1, Knightified and Bishopified 2, Rookified 3 and Queenified 4.

The outlier is Armageddon. It costs 39, which is every point a player can earn in a game (8 Pawns, 2 Knights, 2 Bishops, 2 Rooks and a Queen). It wins the game if your King is your last piece. In practice you can only afford it when you're already close to the position where it wins.

Rules that hold up

Cards that bend chess rules can create positions chess has no answer for. Three rules keep the game playable:

  • Cards beat rules, but legality beats cards. A card wins over normal chess rules, but you can't play a card if the result would be an illegal position. For example, you can't skip your move while in check.
  • One card per turn, and play before you move. Turns stay readable: card, move, draw.
  • When the deck runs out, it's chess. Nobody draws from an empty deck. Once both hands are empty, the game finishes as normal chess.

The look

The first card fronts were colour-coded in blue, red, green and purple. The current version uses one brown frame for every card.

  1. Version 1Colour-coded fronts
  2. CurrentOne frame for all 40 cards

I made a font, Chardss Display, from Cormorant with chess-piece glyphs added. Typing {King} on a card or on the website gives you the King icon. The card data lives in one JSON file, and every push to the game's repository rebuilds the website's rules, card pages and images.

Where it started

The first prototype, from March 2025, was a Python chess engine that plays full chess with castling, en passant and a clock. It never got card mechanics. The game became a physical card deck instead, with the website for rules and card demos.
